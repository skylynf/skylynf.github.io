Abstract

One-step flow maps are increasingly used as posterior samplers, where the point of sampling is the uncertainty that the samples carry. We find that the one-step sampler loses this uncertainty and becomes less stable. Compared with multi-step samples of the same network, its samples lose variance and decorrelate. Supervising the one-step jump directly makes this worse; the samples collapse further toward the conditional mean and break down when training is extended. We trace both failures to the locations where training places its pairs of start and end times. Only pairs on the diagonal of the time triangle receive targets from data, and the pair used for one-step sampling is either left unsupervised or supervised on an island, a set of pairs that the self-consistency identity does not connect to the diagonal, where the objective neither determines the one-step map nor anchors its training.

We bridge the island with an isthmus, which places 5% of the training pairs along the whole edge of the triangle that joins the one-step pair to the diagonal. The error of the one-step map is then bounded by the self-consistency residual along this edge. With the network, the loss and the inference code unchanged and at no added cost, the isthmus improves one-step sampling across several flow-map objectives, in MRI reconstruction, face inpainting, and unconditional generation on CIFAR-10 and ImageNet \(32\times 32\). On brain MRI, it restores one-step calibration to 0.96 and the correlation with multi-step samples to 0.93; both vary far less across seeds, and the agreement holds when training is extended to three times its length.

Key idea

A flow-map network can jump from the source to the data in one step, or it can integrate the same flow in many short steps. If the one-step map is faithful, the two samplers should agree, both in their samples and in their spread. In the paper they do not: on brain MRI, the 64-step sampler of a standard MeanFlow model is close to calibrated, while the one-step samples of the same network are already under-dispersed (calibration \(0.67\), where \(1\) is exact) and only partly correlated with the 64-step samples (\(\rho = 0.58\)). Supervising the one-step jump directly makes this worse and collapses the samples toward the conditional mean (calibration \(0.26\)). When training is extended, the collapsed one-step sampler can break down, while the 64-step sampler of the same network stays intact.

Both symptoms come from the geometry of training pairs. Training draws a start time \(r\) and an end time \(t\) from the triangle \(0 \le r \le t \le 1\). Only the diagonal \(r = t\) receives targets from the data, and the mean-flow identity passes this information along rows of constant end time. One-step sampling is the corner \((r, t) = (0, 1)\), which sits at one end of the landing edge \(t = 1\). The standard time sampler places no training pairs on this edge, so the corner is unsupervised. A pair placed at the corner alone is a supervision island: the identity does not connect it to the diagonal, the one-step map is not uniquely determined (an arbitrary map composed with the true flow still has zero residual), and its training targets rest on untrained pairs.

The remedy is a thin bridge. The error of the one-step map is bounded by the residual of the identity along the landing edge, so training controls this error once its pairs cover the whole edge. An isthmus is the simplest such sampler: with probability \(5\%\), a training pair is replaced by \(r \sim \mathcal{U}(0,1)\), \(t = 1\). Only the time sampler changes.

Method

In its default form the isthmus is a one-line change to any base time sampler with \(r \le t\). With probability \(\pi_{\mathrm{isth}} = 5\%\), the pair is replaced by a jump that starts at a uniform time and lands on the data. The new pairs are trained with the unchanged mean-flow target. Pairs are replaced rather than added, so the cost per iteration is unchanged. The analysis requires only \(\pi_{\mathrm{isth}} > 0\); shares from 2.5% to 10% perform alike in the paper’s ablations. 

r, t = base_sampler(n)        # any sampler, r <= t
isth = rand(n) < 0.05         # 5% of the examples
r = where(isth, rand(n), r)   # start ~ U(0, 1)
t = where(isth, 1.0, t)       # end on the data

Because the base sampler has no pair on the landing edge, the one-step error is bounded by the mean-square residual over the isthmus pairs. A small error implies closeness to the data distribution in Wasserstein-2 distance and agreement with the multi-step samples. Calibration further requires an error that is small relative to the posterior variance, which the bound does not itself provide; it is tested empirically.

Main results

The experiments change only the time sampler. The main setting is posterior sampling for \(8\times\) accelerated MRI on IXI brain slices, with a conditional U-Net trained with the MeanFlow loss for 40k iterations, reported as means over three training seeds. The same comparisons are repeated on fastMRI knee data and CelebA inpainting, and with the official MeanFlow code on unconditional CIFAR-10 and ImageNet \(32\times 32\).

With 64 steps, every network in the comparison is close to calibrated (\(V/B = 0.92\)–\(1.03\)), so the network itself has learned the uncertainty of the posterior. Without an isthmus, the same networks in one step reach only \(V/B = 0.56\)–\(0.84\). With the isthmus, every recipe reaches paired correlation \(\rho \ge 0.93\), and MeanFlow and SNaP become the only one-step samplers that are both consistent with 64 steps and calibrated (\(V/B = 0.96\) and \(0.95\)).

Moving the same 5% of pairs between regions of the triangle isolates the effect of placement. The isthmus is the only region whose one-step samples are both consistent with 64 steps and calibrated, with \(V_1/B_1 = 0.96\) on IXI — the same calibration as 64 steps — and it is also the best region on the knee (\(V_1/B_1 = 0.83\), \(\kappa_{\mathrm{f}} = 0.67\)). Islands fail in the other direction: they can raise correlation but shrink the spread (\(V_1/B_1 = 0.26\) at the corner point, \(0.17\) on the launch strip). The landing strip, which covers only the first 0.3 of the landing edge, comes in second. On CelebA inpainting, the isthmus keeps the one-step spread close to 64 steps (\(V_1/V_{64} = 0.91\)), while the corner point collapses it to 0.37 and later breaks down under longer training.
