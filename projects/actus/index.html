ACTUS

How Uncertain Is My Action? Self-Correcting Visual Planning with Frozen World Models

arXiv PDF Video BibTeX

TL;DR

A frozen world model used as a visual planner assumes every command is executed as sent. At deployment the actuator may apply an unknown actuation shift, and the model has no way to notice. ACTUS (Action Uncertainty Self-correction) runs the same frozen model backwards: for a hypothesized shift it predicts the next observation, and the residual against what actually happened is a shift score. From that score it detects the shift with a Rao test, estimates it with Cramér–Rao error bars, and compensates by replanning through the inverse—without updating any weights. On PushT, self-correction raises success from 0.650 to 0.862, and on Cube from 0.378 to 0.432, matching an oracle told the true shift at the same block. On a real SO-101 arm, a 10° base-joint offset that the encoders miss is estimated at 10.1° from the wrist camera, and median reach error falls from 36–38 mm to 3.0–5.5 mm.

Problem

Visual planning often pairs a frozen latent world model with sampling-based model predictive control. The planner rolls out candidate action sequences, keeps those predicted to reach the goal, and executes the first block. This uses the model only in the forward direction. At deployment, a miscalibrated axis or a slipped joint can silently transform every command. We call this an actuation shift. The frozen model still assumes commands are executed as given, so its preferred plan can drive the robot away from the goal. In other words, the model has no notion of execution uncertainty: it assumes every command is executed as given and cannot detect when it is not.

Existing remedies act on the model’s prediction error, or they update weights or auxiliary modules at test time, without separating an actuation shift from the model’s own error. The starting point here is that a frozen world model is a counterfactual simulator of its own actions. For each hypothesized shift \(\theta\), the model predicts the next latent as if the command had been executed through \(T(\theta)\). Comparing this prediction with the observed next latent reveals which hypothesis best explains the transition, without further training.

Method

A latent world model consists of an encoder and a predictor that maps recent latents and an action block to the next latent. Between the planner and the environment sits an actuator. Under Assumption 1 the executed sub-action is a structured transformation of the commanded one, \(\tilde u_{t,j}=\mathcal{P}(T(\theta)\,u_{t,j})\), with \(\mathcal{P}\) the clipping and \(T(\theta)=\exp\bigl(\sum_{k=1}^{K}\theta_k G_k\bigr)\). The generators \(\{G_k\}\) are linearly independent; the coordinates \(\theta\) are unknown and may drift slowly. For planar increments we take all of \(\mathfrak{gl}(2)\) (rotation, isotropic gain, anisotropic gain, shear; \(K=4\)), and for spatial increments the three axis gains with \(\mathfrak{so}(3)\) (\(K=6\)).

For any shift hypothesis \(\theta\), a counterfactual prediction \(m_t(\theta)\) is the latent the model would have predicted had the commanded block been executed through \(T(\theta)\). The factual observation \(z_{t+1}\) defines a residual \(r_t:=z_{t+1}-m_t(\theta_0)\) around a frozen nominal (zero) point \(\theta_0\). To first order, \(r_t\approx J_t\delta+\varepsilon_t\), where \(\delta\) is the shift relative to \(\theta_0\), \(J_t=\partial m_t/\partial\theta|_{\theta_0}\) is a Jacobian–vector product of \(f\) per generator, and \(\varepsilon_t\) is the model’s own prediction error. Assumption 2 is a calibrated nominal law: under nominal actuation the residual is a zero-mean martingale with covariance estimated from nominal rollouts and then frozen.

The shift score and its information for a transition are

\[ s_t:=J_t^\top\Sigma_t^{-1}r_t,\qquad F_t:=J_t^\top\Sigma_t^{-1}J_t. \]

Over a window or a fleet of \(n\) transitions these pool to \((S,F)\). The score \(s_t\) is a \(K\)-dimensional measurement of \(\delta\) whose own noise is its information. ACTUS uses this statistic in three ways, all without updating the world model:

  • Detect. A Rao statistic \(\Lambda=S^\top F^{-1}S/\kappa\) is compared with a threshold fitted on nominal data. Online, a change-point GLR localizes the onset.
  • Estimate. Gauss–Newton on the same score yields \(\hat\delta\) and Cramér–Rao error bars. Five iterations suffice in every environment tested.
  • Compensate. Once the alarm has fired, every planned block is replaced by \(T(\hat\delta)^{-1}a\), so the executed sub-action equals the planned one whenever \(T(\hat\delta)^{-1}a\) stays in the command box. Compensation is alarm-gated: nominal success is left untouched at the design false-alarm rate.

Theoretical Insights

The residual contains both the shift and the model’s own error. Projecting it onto the \(K\) generator directions gives a score that sees the shift at first order. Prediction-error energy, by contrast, sees a shift only at second order and confounds it with model bias. Numbering below follows the main text.

Proposition 1 (Residual energy confounds the shift with the model’s own error)

Under Assumptions 1–2 and the first-order residual, the change in expected residual energy relative to the nominal predictor splits into a model-bias term, a bias–shift cross term, a residual-scale term, and a quadratic shift term. Only the last term grows with every shift, and it is quadratic: against the shift score, the energy loses a factor \(\sqrt{2}\,\|\delta\|_F/D\) for small shifts.

Intuition. Energy averages the residual over all \(D\) latent directions, of which only \(K\) carry the shift. The score looks only along those \(K\) directions, so a small shift that energy misses is visible at first order.

Proposition 2 (Detectability law)

Under Assumptions 1–2, for a window of \(n\) transitions with mean information \(\bar F\) and \(\mathrm{Cov}(S)=\kappa F\),

\[ \Lambda\sim\chi^2_K(\lambda),\qquad \lambda=\frac{n\|\delta\|_F^2}{\kappa}, \]

asymptotically, including when the planner chooses \(a_t\) from the past. Hence the level-\(\alpha\) power and the window AUROC are universal functions of \(\lambda\) alone,

\[ \beta_K(\lambda)=\mathbb{P}\bigl(\chi^2_K(\lambda)>q_{K,1-\alpha}\bigr),\qquad A_K(\lambda)\approx\Phi\Bigl(\frac{\lambda}{2\sqrt{K}+\lambda}\Bigr), \]

and the test \(\{\Lambda>q_{K,1-\alpha}\}\) is locally asymptotically optimal among invariant tests.

Intuition. Once the Fisher information collected about the shift is known, detection performance is known before any shift is seen. Environments, generators, and sizes collapse onto one curve whose only constants are \(K\) and the over-dispersion \(\kappa\) fitted on nominal data.

Theorem 1 (Detection limits set by the model’s own error)

The model’s error lies in the span of \(J_t\) and is constant over an episode or a recording session. To the detector it looks like a shift, which makes the Rao statistic over-dispersed, more so when episodes are pooled. For a fleet of episodes in one session, the null mean of the Rao statistic grows linearly with the fleet size through a factor \(\kappa(m)\) that is identified from nominal data; a shift smaller than the session variability cannot be detected even from a session, however large the fleet. A nominal reference from the same session removes the session effect and restores information about \(\delta\).

Intuition. Detection cannot outrun the model’s own error. All three variance components that set the threshold are fixed on nominal data before any shift is applied, which is what the real-arm protocol uses.

Theorem 2 (Recovery decomposition)

Let \(S_{\mathrm{nom}}\) be the nominal success rate, \(S^\star_k\) the success of a planner that uses the true shift from block \(k\) (with \(S^\star_0\) the frozen planner), and \(\hat S\) the success of the self-correcting planner that switches at its alarm block \(\tau\) with estimate \(\hat\delta\). Then

\[ S_{\mathrm{nom}}-\hat S=L_{\mathrm{box}}+L_{\mathrm{lat}}+L_{\mathrm{est}}. \]

The three terms have separate causes: the command box, which no correction of the actuator can undo; the blocks planned before the alarm, each costing the drop of the delayed-oracle curve; and the estimation error, which is second order in the Cramér–Rao bound.

Intuition. Once the alarm has fired, little success should remain to be recovered from a better estimate. What compensation cannot return is mostly the box and the wait for the alarm, which is what the Cube decomposition shows.

Key results

Experiments use the released LeWM checkpoints, loaded strictly and never updated, on PushT, TwoRoom, and OGBench-Cube, and repeat the protocol on DINO-WM. PushT and TwoRoom are driven by planar increments with \(K=4\) generators, and Cube by spatial increments with \(K=6\). All methods share weights, planner, replanning schedule, start–goal pairs, and shift draws. Detection uses 124, 70 and 60 shifts on PushT, TwoRoom and Cube. Recovery is the success rate averaged over shifts drawn from a prior, and differences are paired over the same draws.

Detection follows the predicted law

The shift score performs best in all seven settings of Fig. 4a, exceeding the strongest baseline by 0.04–0.21 AUROC except where all statistics saturate. On weak shifts, residual energy achieves only 0.44–0.60 AUROC in each weakest bin. At \(n=20\), measured AUROC differs by at most 0.05 from the theoretical law, and all 254 shifts follow the predicted curves across generators, magnitudes, and tasks (Fig. 1c). Detection delay tracks the prediction from accumulated information, with rank correlation at least 0.95 in every environment (Fig. 4b).

Compensation matches an oracle, without updating weights

The actuator shift costs the frozen planner 22.3 points of success on PushT and 14.2 on Cube. In Table 1, ACTUS recovers 21.2 and 5.4, respectively, without changing model weights or nominal success. It matches Oracle@1, which receives the true shift at the same block, within statistical uncertainty. Five test-time adaptation methods gain at most 2.3 points over Frozen; four fall below it on both tasks, and nominal success drops by up to 6.6 points. Structural baselines isolate the value of actuator modeling: robust planning without estimation loses 12.4 points on PushT, an unstructured affine correction recovers 12.8, and Lie-SGD with the same low-dimensional coordinates recovers 17.9. The remaining Cube gap is mainly attributed to latency and action-box constraints (Fig. 5b), consistent with Theorem 2.

The same procedure transfers to DINO-WM

The PushT and Cube protocols are repeated on a second world model, DINO-WM, whose latent is a grid of frozen DINOv2 patch tokens. All of the components are reused unchanged, and only the read-out space of the residual is model specific. ACTUS transfers without retuning. On the prior draws it gains 18.0 points over the frozen planner on PushT and 7.0 on Cube, level with Oracle@1, with a median estimation error \(\rho\) of 0.04. False alarms on held-out nominal fleets are 0%.

A real SO-101 arm

The same method is tested on a real robot arm, with a world model trained on the arm’s own data. The arm is an SO-101 with a fixed and a wrist camera. One LeWM per camera is trained on nominal data, then frozen, and calibrated on nominal sessions. Shifts are injected in software on the commanded end-effector increment, or at the servo interface as a 10° base-joint offset that the joint readings do not reveal. The task is an open-loop reach to one of eight targets, and the error of an episode is the distance between its end point under shift and that of the same seed without shift.

Across 13 shifts the measured window AUROC tracks the value predicted from nominal data (\(r=0.84\), Fig. 6c). With the base joint 10° off, the wrist camera reads a rotation of 10.1° while the joint readings report none, and compensating with this estimate brings the median reach error from 36–38 mm down to 3.0–5.5 mm, as low as compensating with the true offset and level with the arm’s repeat noise (3.2–5.1 mm; Fig. 6b, top). Under three shifts drawn at random and sealed before the session, ACTUS obtains 92–94% of the error reduction of the true shift (\(p<10^{-11}\)) and leaves 1.9–2.5 mm against a repeat noise of 2.0 mm (Fig. 6b, bottom).
